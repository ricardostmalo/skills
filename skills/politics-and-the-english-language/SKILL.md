# Politics and the English Language

Orwell's argument, 1946: bad prose is not a matter of taste. Stale phrases do your thinking for you, and a writer who reaches for them has stopped deciding what he means. The cure is to think of the thing first and find the words after, instead of letting the words arrive ready-made and hoping they fit.

## The four vices

**Dying metaphors.** Phrases that were once images and are now reflexes: toe the line, ride roughshod, stand shoulder to shoulder, play into the hands of, no axe to grind, Achilles' heel, swan song, hotbed. The writer does not see the picture, so neither does the reader. Either use a fresh image you actually see, or say the plain thing.

**Verbal false limbs.** A simple verb padded into a phrase: render inoperative, militate against, make contact with, be subjected to, give rise to, have the effect of, play a leading part in, take effect, serve the purpose of. Also the passive where the active was available, and noun constructions in place of gerunds ("by examination of" for "by examining"). Cut the limb and use the verb.

**Pretentious diction.** Words chosen to dress a plain statement in science or dignity: phenomenon, element, objective, categorical, effective, virtual, basic, utilize, eliminate, liquidate, exploit, promote, constitute. Foreign phrases used for prestige. Jargon adopted from a field the writer is not in. Say it in the words you would use if you were not trying to impress.

**Meaningless words.** Words with no agreed meaning that let the writer avoid one: in art criticism, "romantic," "plastic," "values," "sentimental," "vitality"; in politics, "democracy," "freedom," "justice," "realistic" used as praise or blame with no definition. If a word can be used by both sides of an argument to mean opposite things, decide what you mean and say that.

## The six rules

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

## Procedure

1. Before writing a sentence, ask what you are trying to say. If you cannot picture it, you are not ready to write it.
2. Write it in the plainest words that carry the picture.
3. Then go back through and hunt the four vices. Mark each one.
4. For each mark, ask Orwell's questions: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect? Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything avoidably ugly?
5. Apply rule 6 last. If the plain version is worse, keep the other.

## What this is for

Orwell's point is not style for its own sake. Vague prose is where dishonesty hides: the passive that names no actor, the abstraction that covers a concrete thing nobody would defend if it were named. Plain writing is harder to lie in. That is why the rules matter beyond making text pleasant.

## Example

Before: "It is generally recognized that the utilization of appropriate methodologies can have the effect of facilitating improved outcomes in the majority of cases."

Vices: passive with no actor ("it is generally recognized"), false limbs ("have the effect of facilitating"), pretentious diction ("utilization," "methodologies," "outcomes"), and no picture anywhere.

After: "If you use the right method, it usually works."
