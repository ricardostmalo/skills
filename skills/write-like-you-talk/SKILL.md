# Write like you talk

Read the essay itself from `sources/pg-write-like-you-talk.txt` (run `scripts/fetch-sources.sh` once). This file is the procedure; the essay is the argument.

Most people switch into a different voice when they write. The written voice is stiffer, uses words they would never say, and makes the writer feel they are conveying more than they are. Graham's claim is that the spoken voice is almost always better, and that using it puts you ahead of most writers because almost nobody does it.

He gives the example of a biographer calling Picasso "the mercurial Spaniard." Nobody would say that out loud. Books are full of it.

The hard cases are not the exception. Specialists explaining difficult things to each other talk plainly. Complexity in the ideas does not require complexity in the sentences.

## The test

Go through the draft one sentence at a time and ask: would I say this to a friend? If not, replace it with what you would say.

That is the whole method. The rest of this file is how to apply it.

## Procedure

1. Draft however you draft.
2. Read each sentence and imagine saying it across a table to someone you like and respect. Mark every sentence that fails.
3. For each marked sentence, say the idea out loud (or as close as you can get), then write down what you said.
4. If the draft is so formal that step 3 fails everywhere, throw the draft out. Explain the whole thing to a friend, verbally, and write that down instead.
5. Read the result aloud. Fix anything that trips the tongue, including things that are grammatically fine but phonetically awkward.

## What tends to fail the test

- Words you know but do not use: "utilize," "commence," "endeavor," "leverage," "facilitate."
- Noun phrases where you would say a verb: "the implementation of" for "when we built."
- Epithets and appositives: "the veteran investor," "the Stanford-educated founder."
- Sentences that open with a frame instead of a claim: "It is worth noting that," "In terms of."
- Passive voice where you know who did it.
- Any sentence that exists to sound like writing.

## What survives the test

- Contractions.
- Short sentences next to long ones, because that is how speech goes.
- Starting a sentence with "and," "but," or "so."
- Saying "I think" when you think it.
- Plain intensifiers: "a lot," "really," "very."
- Repeating a word instead of reaching for a synonym.

## Limits

Talking is not the same as transcribing. Speech has false starts, "um," and repetition that you would cut on the page. Keep the register of speech, not the noise. And some documents (contracts, specs, formal notices) are supposed to sound like writing; leave those alone.

## Example

Before: "The organization's decision to pursue a remote-first operational model was driven by a desire to access a broader talent pool."

Say it to a friend: "We went remote because we couldn't hire enough people locally."

That is the sentence.
