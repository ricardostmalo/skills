# Good writing

Paul Graham's claim: there are two kinds of good writing, sentences that sound good and ideas that are right, and they turn out to be the same thing more often than they should. When you rewrite a sentence to sound better, you almost never make its idea worse, and you often make it better. So sound is a usable test for truth.

This skill is that test, applied.

## Why it works

Rewriting for sound is like shaking a bin of objects: an arbitrary constraint that forces the contents into a better arrangement. When a sentence does not sound right, the usual cause is that the idea under it is not quite right either: it is vague, or it says slightly more than you know, or it skips a step. Fixing the sound makes you find and fix the idea.

The writer is the first reader. Prose that reads easily lets the writer notice problems that stiff prose hides.

## Procedure

1. Read the draft aloud, or as close to aloud as you can. Do not read for meaning. Read for where you stumble.
2. At each stumble, do not fix the words yet. Ask what you meant. Nine times in ten the answer is a more precise idea than the one on the page. Write that.
3. Cut anything that exists to sound authoritative rather than to say something. If a sentence would survive on a slide, it is probably fine. If it would survive only in a press release, cut it.
4. Shorten. Graham found that when forced to cut a passage to fit a layout, it got better, not worse. Treat every paragraph as if it must lose a fifth of its words and see what you drop first. Usually you should have dropped it anyway.
5. Read it aloud again. Repeat until nothing catches.

## What "sounds good" means here

Not meter. The rhythm of good prose is the rhythm of thought: a short claim, a longer explanation, a pause, the next claim. Sentences vary in length because ideas vary in size. A passage where every sentence is the same length sounds wrong even if every sentence is correct, and the wrongness is real: it means the writer was not thinking, only producing.

Signs a sentence does not sound right, and what is usually underneath:

- You have to reread it. The idea has two parts and the sentence pretends it has one.
- It ends on a soft word ("effectively," "in many cases," "going forward"). The writer is hedging a claim they have not actually examined.
- It starts with a throat clear ("It is worth noting that"). There is no claim, only the promise of one.
- Two sentences in a row start the same way. The writer stopped listening.
- It uses a word you would not say out loud. The word is doing work the idea should be doing.

## Limits

This works when the writing is developing ideas. It does not apply to writing that reports fixed facts (a changelog, a legal notice, a spec) where sounding good is not the goal and the ideas are not yours to improve. Do not smooth those; make them exact.

## Example

Before: "It is important to recognize that the implementation of the new onboarding flow has had a meaningful impact on activation rates across cohorts."

Read aloud: stumbles at "important to recognize," again at "implementation of," again at "meaningful impact." What did I mean?

After: "The new onboarding flow raised activation from 31% to 44%. That held across every cohort we tried it on."

The rewrite is shorter and sounds better. It is also more true, because writing it forced me to look up the numbers.
