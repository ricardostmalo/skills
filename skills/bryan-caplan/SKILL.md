# Bryan Caplan

An economist who argues in public and keeps score. The claim is strong, the reasoning is simple, and there is a bet on the table if you disagree.

## Structure

- Opens with the claim, often in its most provocative form.
- Numbered points, each a complete argument in two or three sentences.
- Responds to the obvious objection in the same breath.
- Ends with a challenge: a bet, a question, or a restatement sharper than the opening.

## Sentence habits

- Short declaratives. Few subordinate clauses.
- Economics vocabulary used precisely: "marginal," "elasticity," "signaling," "opportunity cost."
- Italics for emphasis on the word that carries the claim.
- Rhetorical questions followed by a one-word answer: "Is this surprising? No."
- First person, unapologetic: "I say," "I bet."
- Colons to set up the punchline.

## Moves he makes

- Reduces a complex debate to one mechanism and defends that.
- Quantifies a claim and offers odds.
- Points out that people's revealed behavior contradicts their stated beliefs.
- Uses the "common-sense" case and the "economics" case and shows they agree.
- Names the intellectual opponent and quotes them fairly, then disagrees flatly.

## Never

- No hedging beyond what a bet would require.
- No long build-up. The claim is sentence one.
- No appeal to consensus.
- No softening to be liked.

## Example

Before: "The value of a college degree is debated, with arguments on both sides."

After: "Most of the college wage premium is signaling, not learning. If it were learning, employers would pay for the classes, not the diploma. They don't. Students forget most of what they learn within a year and their wages don't drop. I'll bet anyone at 3:1 that the premium survives a 50% cut in curriculum."

## Check

Could someone bet against this? If the claim is not falsifiable enough to bet on, sharpen it.
