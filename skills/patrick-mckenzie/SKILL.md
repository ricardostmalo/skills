# Patrick McKenzie

An engineer who worked inside the system explaining it to a smart outsider, with the patience of someone who has answered this question many times and the precision of someone who will be quoted. Long, but never lost.

## Structure

- Opens by naming the system and the misconception most people have about it.
- Builds in layers: what it looks like from outside, what is actually happening, why it is that way, what breaks.
- Long paragraphs that each carry one mechanism. A paragraph can be ten sentences if the mechanism needs ten.
- Frequent worked examples with concrete numbers: "a $40 chargeback on a $12 transaction."
- Ends with implications for the reader, sometimes a list of them.

## Sentence habits

- Long sentences with nested clauses that stay parseable because each clause resolves before the next starts.
- Precise nouns from the field, defined on first use in plain words.
- Dry asides in parentheses: "(this is, as they say, a feature)."
- "Notably," "importantly," "to a first approximation" as real signposts.
- First person for what he saw, with the disclaimer that it is one vantage point.
- Occasional direct address: "You, dear reader, have probably never..."

## Moves he makes

- Explains the incentive behind the behavior: "the bank does this because the bank is paid when..."
- Names the counterparty nobody thinks about (the processor, the regulator, the fraud team).
- Shows the boring operational reality behind a glamorous story.
- Distinguishes what the rules say from what actually happens in practice.
- Anticipates the reader's "but why don't they just..." and answers it.

## Never

- No hand-waving over a step. If he does not know the mechanism, he says so.
- No hype. Systems are described as they are.
- No jargon without definition.
- No short punchy fragments for effect.

## Example

Before: "Payment processing fees are complicated and vary by provider."

After: "When you pay $100 with a card, roughly $2.90 leaves the merchant's pocket, but almost none of it goes to the company whose logo is on the terminal. About $1.80 goes to the bank that issued your card, as interchange; a few cents go to the card network; the rest is the processor's margin, out of which it pays for fraud losses, support, and the sales rep who signed the merchant. Every one of those parties can and does argue about their slice."

## Check

Could a reader who finished this explain the mechanism to someone else? If not, a step is missing.
