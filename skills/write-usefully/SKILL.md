# Write usefully

Read the essay itself from `sources/pg-how-to-write-usefully.txt` (run `scripts/fetch-sources.sh` once). This file is the procedure; the essay is the argument.

Useful writing tells people something true and important that they did not already know, and tells it as unequivocally as possible. Graham breaks that into four things multiplied together, so a zero in any one kills the piece.

## The four

**Importance.** How many people it affects, times how much. Use yourself as the proxy: if the topic matters a lot to you, it probably matters to enough readers. Start narrow; you can be the expert on a small thing and the audience finds you.

**Novelty.** The reader did not know it before. This is easier than it sounds. Much of the most useful writing says something the reader half-knew and had never put into words. Anything that surprised you when you noticed it will surprise them.

**Correctness.** Not false. The way to be correct without being vague is to make the claim as strong as you can and then qualify it exactly as much as the truth requires. Vague writing is one failure; bold-and-wrong is the other. The target is bold and true.

**Strength.** Say it as directly as the truth allows. Strength is not volume. It is the absence of padding around a claim that can stand on its own.

## Qualifications

Qualifiers are not weakness. They carry information: how sure you are, how widely the claim applies, where you got it. "I think" is honest when you think it; it tells the reader the claim is yours and not yet settled. What ruins writing is qualifying every sentence the same amount, which tells the reader nothing about which claims are solid.

So: qualify precisely. A claim you are sure of gets none. A claim you are fairly sure of gets one word. A claim you are guessing at says so.

## Procedure

1. Write down, in one sentence, the thing the reader will know after reading that they did not know before. If you cannot, there is no essay yet.
2. Ask whether it matters to you. If it does not, pick something else.
3. Draft. Make every claim as strong as you believe it.
4. Go back through and add qualification only where the truth demands it. Remove qualification where it is there for cover.
5. Cut. When a sentence is a problem, delete it before trying to fix it. When a section is a problem, delete the section. Reread until nothing catches.
6. Check the ending. It should end when the idea is done, on the last thing that is true, not on a summary.

## Signs the piece is not useful yet

- You cannot state the novel claim in one sentence.
- Every paragraph has the same level of hedging.
- The strongest sentence is in the middle and the ending is a restatement.
- A reader who already agreed would learn nothing.
- It would be improved by deleting the first paragraph. (Try it.)

## Example

Weak: "There are many factors that may contribute to startup failure, and founders should be aware of the various challenges they might face."

Useful: "Most startups die because they make something nobody wants. The other causes are real but rare, and founders spend far too much time worrying about them."

The second one could be wrong. That is what makes it useful: it says something, so it can be checked.
