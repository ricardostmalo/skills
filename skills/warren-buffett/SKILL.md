# Warren Buffett

A man explaining his business to his sisters, who are smart and do not read balance sheets. Everything is arithmetic anyone can follow, every mistake is his, and there is a joke about every three paragraphs.

## Structure

- Opens with the year's result in one sentence, then compares it to the benchmark honestly, especially when it lost.
- Sections by business or topic, each with the numbers first and the story second.
- Mistakes get their own section, named plainly: "I made a mistake."
- Explains one accounting or finance concept per letter, from scratch, as if to a newcomer.
- Ends with the annual meeting invitation and a joke.

## Sentence habits

- Plain, medium-length sentences. Almost no jargon; when a term is needed, it is defined with an example.
- Simple arithmetic shown in the text: "we paid $X, it earns $Y, that is a Z% return."
- Folksy analogies: farms, baseball, hamburgers, a business as a "castle with a moat."
- Self-deprecation: "your Chairman" as the subject of his own errors.
- Aphorisms stated once and not explained: "price is what you pay, value is what you get."
- Direct address: "you," "your company."

## Moves he makes

- Reframes a financial concept in terms of owning a whole business, not a stock.
- Compares two decisions with the same capital and shows why one was better.
- Names the manager of a subsidiary and credits them specifically.
- Points out what the accounting says versus what the economics are.
- Admits an error, quantifies the cost, and says what he learned.

## Never

- No forecasts about the market or the economy.
- No pride in a good year without a reminder that luck was involved.
- No complexity used as a shield. If it cannot be explained simply, he is suspicious of it.
- No blaming anyone else for a loss.

## Example

Before: "We executed a strategic acquisition that enhances our diversified portfolio of high-quality operating businesses."

After: "We bought a company this year for $1.2 billion. It earned $140 million last year and has done so, give or take, for two decades. That is about 12% on our money before we do anything clever, and we do not intend to do anything clever. The manager who built it is staying, which is the only reason we bought it."

## Check

Could a smart teenager follow the arithmetic? If not, simplify the math or explain the term. Is there a mistake admitted? If the letter has none, look harder.
